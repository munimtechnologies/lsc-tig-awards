# lsc-tig-awards

Every **Legal Services Corporation Technology Initiative Grant (TIG)** from 2021 to 2025, one record per award, as LSC printed it, in one machine-readable file. Each record notes whether LSC's own project description mentions **AI** and whether it mentions **intake**.

TIG is the main federal source of technology money for civil legal aid in the US. LSC publishes each year's awards as a press release or a web page, and its archive for 2000-2020 is an Airtable view. We found no machine-readable list of the 2021-2025 rounds and no published count of how much of the money goes to AI projects. LSC's February 2026 technology report, *The Next Frontier: Harnessing Technology to Close the Justice Gap* (https://www.lsc.gov/techreport), names AI projects it has funded. Going by LawNext's coverage of it, the report gives no count of AI awards or AI dollars.

## What it shows

AI went from one award in 2021 to more than 40% of TIG dollars in 2024 and 2025.

| 2022 | 33 | 2 | 6.1% | $4,679,135 | $261,418 | 5.6% | 1 |
| 2023 | 33 | 4 | 12.1% | $5,157,033 | $1,089,634 | 21.1% | 1 |
| 2024 | 32 | 9 | 28.1% | $6,306,081 | $2,720,420 | 43.1% | 2 |
| 2025 | 32 | 12 | 37.5% | $4,064,308 | $1,643,948 | 40.4% | 8 |

The table is generated by `python tig.py`, and the tests check that it matches the data.

- **AI intake took off in the 2025 round.** Eight of the 32 awards announced in December 2025 put AI into intake, screening or the intake hotline: Florida Rural Legal Services (an AI voice hotline with Safe Haven AI), Legal Aid Society of Middle Tennessee and the Cumberlands (agentic AI completing phone intakes), Southern Minnesota Regional Legal Services (generative AI in Minnesota's statewide intake), Legal Aid Services of Oregon, Legal Services of Eastern Missouri (an AI-driven intake plan), Legal Aid of the Bluegrass, Legal Services of the Hudson Valley and Legal Aid of Southeastern Pennsylvania. Together they hold **$1,046,054**, a quarter of the dollars listed for 2025. Only four AI intake awards came in the four earlier rounds combined.
- These projects will be evaluated in 2026 and 2027, because every TIG requires an evaluation plan. Two of them, LASMTC and FRLS, jointly asked for an independent evaluator in September 2026. A shared way to measure AI intake is in [legal-aid-intake-eval](https://github.com/munimtechnologies/legal-aid-intake-eval).
- `python tig.py --ai 2025` lists each year's AI awards with the words LSC used.

What this does and doesn't say: only LSC grantees can apply for a TIG, and LSC publishes the awards, not the applications. So a state with no award may have had no applicant or unfunded applicants, and the data can't tell which. Statewide and national projects funded elsewhere, such as shared websites or form tools, may still reach people in these states. This section shows where the awards went, not who was served.

## TIG money against basic field money, by state

LSC splits its main grant money, **basic field** funding, among states by the number of people in poverty in each one (Census figures). TIG money is awarded by competition. This section asks how far the two differ: for each state, its share of all TIG dollars listed for 2021-2025 divided by its share of LSC's 2025 basic field funding. An index of 1.0 means a state got TIG money in proportion to its poverty population. 0.5 means half its share, and 2.0 means double.

The two distributions differ a lot. Of the 56 jurisdictions with basic field money (50 states, DC and five territories), the 12 with no TIG in 2021-2025 (seven states and five territories) hold 10.7% of basic field money. The 23 with an index under 0.5 hold 39.4% of basic field money and received 9.3% of TIG dollars; they include California and New York, the largest and fourth-largest basic field recipients (10.8% and 6.2% of basic field money, 4.7% and 1.2% of TIG dollars). At the other end, 11 jurisdictions with an index of 2.0 or more (DC, DE, KS, MI, MN, MO, MT, ND, NH, VT, WV) hold 8.8% of basic field money and received 33.5% of TIG dollars. The five highest indexes are Vermont (22.0), Montana (13.4), DC (12.1), New Hampshire (5.7) and North Dakota (4.7): each holds under 0.4% of basic field money against TIG totals of $280,000 to $1.1 million. Seven jurisdictions sit between 0.8 and 1.2 (FL, OH, PA, WA, MA, CT, OK).

What this does and doesn't say:

- It compares five rounds of TIG awards (2021-2025) with **one** year of basic field money (2025). Each state's share of basic field money moves slowly: LSC says the poverty estimates it allocates by change only every three years. But it is not fixed.
- A state's basic field share is close to, not exactly, its poverty share. LSC's [allocation rule](https://www.lsc.gov/grants/basic-field-grant/basic-field-allocation-process) sets aside a fixed 2.802% of the basic field appropriation for Native American service areas and splits the rest among general and agricultural worker areas by their share of the US poverty population (American Community Survey for general areas, Decennial Census for American Samoa, Guam and the Virgin Islands, Department of Labor figures for agricultural worker areas). The next section divides by people in poverty instead.
- The 2025 amounts are LSC's **estimates** from November 2024, made before the final FY2025 appropriation.
- 2025 is the newest year available this way. LSC published a Notice of Intent to Award in the Federal Register every autumn from 2016 to 2024, but as of 16 October 2026 there is none for 2026 grants (Federal Register API search of LSC documents).
- Basic field money here includes all three kinds of service area: general, Native American (codes starting with N) and agricultural worker (codes starting with M). The two multi-state agricultural worker areas count in the state LSC printed for them: MMX-1 (Pine Tree Legal Assistance) in Maine and MSX-2 (Texas RioGrande Legal Aid) in Texas. TN-4 names two alternative applicants, and its money counts in Tennessee either way.
- US territories have basic field money and no TIG in these rounds, so they appear with an index of 0.
- As in the section above, these are awards, not applications. A low index can mean nobody applied, and a statewide or national project funded in one state may also serve others.
- Awards with no published amount (one in 2025) are left out of the TIG dollars, as in the first table.

## TIG dollars per person in poverty, by state

The section above compares TIG money with basic field **dollars**. Those dollars include LSC's fixed set-aside for Native American service areas, which is not divided by poverty population. This section divides by the **people in poverty** LSC funds against instead, and adds TIG dollars per person in poverty.

LSC does not publish a per-area poverty table we could fetch as data, but its page of [2027 service areas subject to competition](https://www.lsc.gov/grants/basic-field-grant/lsc-service-areas/2027-service-areas-subject-competition) prints the poverty population of 46 general and agricultural worker areas. Dividing their 2025 amounts from the Federal Register notice by those populations gives one rate, **$11.8586 per person in poverty**. Each of the 46 printed populations comes back from its 2025 amount at that rate to within one person. The same page's 2027 estimates are the 2025 amounts times 0.9616, to within $1 in all 60 areas it lists, Native American areas included. Applying the rate to every other 2025 amount gives the poverty population of all 163 general and agricultural worker areas (`data/poverty_areas_2025.json`, 46 printed by LSC and 117 derived).

Across the 56 jurisdictions, that is 42,301,792 people and 58 cents of TIG money per person in poverty over five rounds. The spread is wide. Vermont received $12.39 per person in poverty, about 300 times Virginia's 4 cents. The 20 jurisdictions with an index under 0.5 hold 36.0% of the poverty population and received 7.5% of TIG dollars. The 11 with an index of 2.0 or more (DC, DE, KS, MI, MN, MO, MT, ND, NH, VT, WV) hold 8.8% and received 33.5%. The 12 with no TIG in 2021-2025 hold 10.9% of the poverty population. California (10.8% of people in poverty, 4.7% of TIG dollars), Texas (10.1%, 5.6%) and New York (6.4%, 1.2%) are the three largest.

Counting people instead of dollars moves the states with large Native American service areas. Alaska goes from 0.37 to 0.62, Arizona from just under 0.50 to 0.71 and South Dakota from 0.28 to 0.58, so all three leave the under-0.5 group, which shrinks from 23 to 20. North Dakota (4.67 to 6.64), Montana (13.38 to 15.03), Hawaii (1.54 to 1.76) and Oklahoma (0.84 to 0.95) rise too. The dollar index counted their Native American set-aside money in their basic field share, which made that share larger than their share of people in poverty and their index smaller.

What this does and doesn't say:

- The per-area poverty populations are LSC's own figures, recovered from its published dollars. They are not new data: LSC's own service area pages show them, in tables loaded by script. What is new here is a machine-readable copy for all 163 areas and the comparison with TIG money.
- 117 of the 163 populations are derived, on the assumption that every general and agricultural worker area is funded at the one rate the 46 printed areas share. The 46 are in 26 states and the Virgin Islands, and all of them fit it to within one person, but the derived figures were not checked one by one.
- The MP row is LSC's Micronesia service area. - The 2027 page does not say which year's poverty estimates it uses. LSC updates them every three years.
- Agricultural worker populations come from Department of Labor figures, not the Census, and LSC's pages do not say whether they overlap with the general areas' counts. Both count here.
- Native American service areas are left out of the people column because LSC funds them from a fixed set-aside. TIG awards to Native American grantees still count in their state's TIG dollars, which raises the index of any state where a Native American grantee won a TIG.
- The limits of the section above still hold: awards, not applications; five rounds against one year; multi-state projects counted once; one 2025 award with no published amount left out.

## Pro Bono Innovation Fund awards, and what LSC's awards page leaves out

LSC's second competitive grant program, the **Pro Bono Innovation Fund (PBIF)**, pays legal aid programs to bring volunteer lawyers into their work. Like TIG, only LSC grantees can apply. This section adds the 2022-2025 PBIF rounds and sets them against basic field money in the same way as the TIG sections.

The obvious source is LSC's [PBIF Awards page](https://www.lsc.gov/grants-grantee-resources/our-grant-programs/pro-bono-innovation-fund/awards). It prints 66 award headings for 2022-2025 (14 Planning, 25 Project, 14 Sustainability and 13 Transformation grants) totalling $18,106,525.14. Checked against LSC's own yearly press releases, the page is **incomplete for 2022 and 2023**:

- The [2022 release](https://www.lsc.gov/press-release/2022-pro-bono-grants-totaling-475-million-awarded-legal-aid-organizations) announces 15 grants to 15 organizations and prints an amount for each; they sum to $4,754,551, matching its "$4.75 million". The page lists 8 of them. What this does and doesn't say:

- The release-only grants come from LSC's own press releases, not from a guess. Their amounts are whole dollars as the releases print them, and they carry no track or subject.
- Where the two sources disagree on an amount, we can't tell which is right. The differences are small next to the totals: about $16,000 or less each, under $34,000 in all, against $20.8 million.
- The 2024 release prints no amounts and the 2025 release prints three. For those years the page is the only full list; it agrees with the counts LSC stated.
- PBIF rounds run 2022-2025 here and TIG rounds 2021-2025, so the combined table mixes four rounds with five.
- LSC's [October 2026 release](https://www.lsc.gov/press-release/lsc-awards-415m-pro-bono-legal-services-across-18-states) announces 2026 PBIF grants (more than $4.15 million to 20 organizations in 18 states) but prints only some amounts, and the awards page did not list them on 16 October 2026. They are not included.
- LSC says it has made 200 PBIF grants totalling more than $55 million since 2014. Earlier rounds are not included.
- A single grant moves a small jurisdiction's index a long way: the Virgin Islands' 22.13 is one $204,429 grant. Read the index for small jurisdictions with that in mind.
- The other limits of the TIG sections hold: awards, not applications; one year of basic field money; multi-state projects counted once, in the grantee's state. Each grantee's state is the state of its general service areas in LSC's 2025 basic field notice.

## How the flags are set

- `mentions_ai` is true when LSC's description of the project names AI: the word "AI", "artificial intelligence", "generative", "chatbot" or an AI product such as Microsoft Copilot. "Automated" or "automation" alone does not count, so automated forms, texting and scheduling are not AI here. Neither is a product name such as "Legal Aid Content Intelligence" when nothing else says AI. Every AI record carries the fragment of LSC's text that triggered it (`ai_terms`), so each call can be checked.
- `mentions_intake` is true when the description mentions intake, screening, triage or a hotline.
- Amounts are copied award by award. Two independent passes over each LSC page gave the same names and amounts.

## Limits

- The flags describe LSC's one-paragraph summaries, not the projects themselves. A project summarised as "potential AI features" counts the same as one built around a model. Projects that use AI but are not described that way are missed.
- Eleven 2024 awards (Technology Improvement Projects) were printed with an amount and no description. They count as not mentioning AI.
- LSC's 2025 page lists 31 awards, not the 32 its text announces. The 32nd, Legal Services of Greater Miami ("expanding its use of AI agents to automate administrative processes"), appears only in the press release, with no amount. It is counted as an AI award and left out of both dollar totals.
- LSC's printed totals do not always add up. For 2024, the listed amounts sum to $6,306,081, while the release says $6.2 million and its headline $5.9 million. For 2021, the list is $7 under the stated $4,268,938. The dollar columns use the amounts as listed. 2025 is likewise under the stated "over $4.2 million", partly because of the missing amount.
- LSC says the 2025 awards went to organizations in 22 states. The 2025 records here cover 21. We could not find the 22nd.
- Oklahoma Indian Legal Services is printed with "(OH)" on LSC's 2025 page and recorded here as OK.
- State counts use the state LSC printed for each grantee, so a program serving several states counts once, in its home state.
- Award years are rounds (announced each November or December). Projects usually run 18 to 24 months after that.
